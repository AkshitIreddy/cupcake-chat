# Reading notes and calculation assumptions

These are preparation notes checked on 15 September 2026. Numerical scenarios below are declared assumptions, not measurements from an anime or records of an ancient army. Do not invent quotations, citations, dates, specific historic rations, or a universal human g-tolerance threshold.

## ODM gear: physical constraints

Use total person-plus-equipment mass 80 kg and g = 9.81 m/s². Circular-motion radial acceleration is v²/r. At 20 m/s and radii 5, 10, 20 m it is 80, 40, 20 m/s² (8.15, 4.08, 2.04 g respectively). These are radial accelerations, not automatically harness g-loads. At the bottom of a vertical arc, for a single upward resultant cable force and no other force except gravity, T = m(v²/r + g). At 20 m/s and r=10 m, T=3,984.8 N, about 5.08 times body-plus-gear weight. Geometry, additional forces, duration, onset, and restraint change the result.

At 20 m/s, kinetic energy is 16,000 J. Gaining 10 m altitude adds 7,848 J. From rest, ending at that height and speed, the ideal net mechanical energy increase is 23,848 J; over 2 s the average useful mechanical power is 11,924 W. This excludes losses and does not specify a motor or fuel. Energy capacity and peak power delivery differ. Doubling speed quadruples kinetic energy and radial acceleration at fixed radius. A cable can redirect velocity without providing all its kinetic energy; do not confuse tension force with motor power. Power is force dotted with velocity at the relevant point, not force alone.

A point-mass calculation cannot validate hook retention, walls/trees, reel dynamics, cable fatigue, body rotation, collisions, drag, gas supply, or a wearable human system. A rated breaking strength is not an allowable operating load. State feasibility as a reasoned assessment with limitations, not a prototype claim or a build recipe.

## Historical context

Roman supply systems varied with place and period. Evidence from Roman Britain describes imports through ports, local production, and changing regional supply patterns. Archaeological evidence survives unevenly; durable pottery is more visible than most food and textile consignments.
Source: English Heritage https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/story-of-england/romans/commerce/

Viking voyages included raiding, trading, and settlement. Shipbuilding and maritime skills enabled their connections; avoid presenting every Scandinavian as a raider or inventing ship capacities.
Sources: British Museum https://www.britishmuseum.org/vikings-live and https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/galleries/sutton-hoo-and-europe

Horses were central to Mongol mobility, and pastoral nomadism involved herding and seasonal movement. This does not imply unlimited pasture, identical remount counts, zero supply needs, or one explanation for every campaign.
Sources: Metropolitan Museum https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2000/mongolian-horse and https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2002/nomadic-art

For fictional logistics problems, calculate from the user's assumptions. Siege: 18,000 × 0.9 = 16,200 kg usable. 600 × 0.75 = 450 kg/day gives 36 days. After ten full days, 11,700 kg remains. With 720 people at 0.75 kg/day, use is 540 kg/day, so 21.6667 further days (31.6667 total). Voyage: 40 × 7 × 1.2 = 336 kg; 20% reserve gives 403.2 kg. Reserve 67.2 kg covers 1.4 days at 48 kg/day. Ten days need 480 kg; deficit is 76.8 kg.
