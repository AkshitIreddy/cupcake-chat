# Historical reading notes and calculation assumptions

Preparation notes checked on 15 September 2026. Numerical scenarios are fictional assumptions, not
documented army rations.

## Historical context

Roman supply systems varied with place and period. Evidence from Roman Britain describes imports
through ports, local production, and changing regional supply patterns. Archaeological evidence
survives unevenly; durable pottery is more visible than most food and textile consignments. Source:
English Heritage https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/learn/story-of-england/romans/commerce/

Viking voyages included raiding, trading, and settlement. Shipbuilding and maritime skills enabled
their connections; avoid presenting every Scandinavian as a raider or inventing ship capacities.
Sources: British Museum https://www.britishmuseum.org/vikings-live and
https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/galleries/sutton-hoo-and-europe

Horses were central to Mongol mobility, and pastoral nomadism involved herding and seasonal
movement. This does not imply unlimited pasture, identical remount counts, zero supply needs, or one
explanation for every campaign. Sources: Metropolitan Museum
https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2000/mongolian-horse and
https://www.metmuseum.org/exhibitions/listings/2002/nomadic-art

For fictional logistics problems, calculate from the user's assumptions. Siege: 18,000 × 0.9 =
16,200 kg usable. 600 × 0.75 = 450 kg/day gives 36 days. After ten full days, 11,700 kg remains.
With 720 people at 0.75 kg/day, use is 540 kg/day, so 21.6667 further days (31.6667 total). Voyage:
40 × 7 × 1.2 = 336 kg; 20% reserve gives 403.2 kg. Reserve 67.2 kg covers 1.4 days at 48 kg/day. Ten
days need 480 kg; deficit is 76.8 kg.
